02

FOUNDATION

What is Artificial Intelligence?

2.1 The intelligent-agent definition

AI
COURSE DEFINITION

Artificial Intelligence is the synthesis and analysis of computational agents that act intelligently.

This definition is important because it focuses on agents and their behaviour, rather than defining AI as simply making computers look or behave like humans.

An agent is something that acts in an environment. It receives information about what is happening and performs actions that influence its surroundings.

2.2 What makes an agent intelligent?

According to the introductory lecture, an agent acts intelligently when its actions are appropriate for its goals and circumstances. The lecture also highlights flexibility, learning and the limitations under which an agent makes decisions.

Appropriate actions

The action should make sense in relation to the agent's goals and its current situation.

Example: A navigation system recommends a route that moves the driver towards the destination.

Flexibility

The agent should be able to respond when the environment or goals change.

Example: A navigation system changes its recommendation when a road is closed.

Learning from experience

An agent may improve its performance using information gained from experience.

Example: A spam detector can improve its classification using additional labelled examples.

Limited resources

Decisions are made using available perceptual information and computational resources.

Example: A robot must make decisions using the information its sensors can currently provide.

2.3 Narrow AI versus Artificial General Intelligence

The lecture distinguishes AI developed for a particular problem from a hypothetical system with general human-level intellectual capability.

Aspect Narrow AI Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)
Purpose Designed for a specific problem or task. Would understand or learn a broad range of intellectual tasks.
Scope Limited to its intended task or domain. General-purpose intellectual ability.
Course position The focus of practical narrow-AI techniques. Presented in the lecture as hypothetical.
Example A system that identifies spam emails. A hypothetical agent able to learn many different intellectual tasks.

2.4 The two goals of AI

Scientific goal

Understand the principles that make intelligent behaviour possible in natural and artificial systems.

  • Analyse natural and artificial agents.
  • Formulate and test hypotheses.
  • Experiment with computational systems.

Engineering goal

Design and build useful intelligent artefacts that solve real problems.

  • Construct intelligent systems.
  • Apply AI methods to practical problems.
  • Evaluate whether the system is useful.
Exam distinction: The scientific goal is about understanding intelligent behaviour. The engineering goal is about building useful intelligent systems. Do not treat the two as interchangeable.
03

AI PERSPECTIVES

The Four Approaches to AI

AI can be studied using two independent distinctions: whether we are interested in thinking or acting, and whether the behaviour should imitate humans or follow rational principles.

3.1 Understand the two dimensions

DIMENSION 1

Thinking versus acting

Thinking focuses on internal reasoning or mental processes. Acting focuses on observable behaviour.

DIMENSION 2

Human versus rational

Human-like behaviour imitates people. Rational behaviour aims to make sensible decisions towards a goal.

3.2 The four approaches compared

Approach Main concern Central question
Thinking humanly Human thought processes Does the machine think like a human?
Acting humanly Human-like behaviour Does the machine behave like a human?
Thinking rationally Correct reasoning Does the machine reason correctly?
Acting rationally Goal-directed action Does the machine choose an appropriate action?

3.3 Thinking humanly

Thinking humanly attempts to model the way humans actually think. It is closely related to cognitive science because it requires us to understand processes such as memory, learning, decision-making and problem-solving.

The lecture describes several ways of studying human thinking:

  • Introspection: examining our own thought processes.
  • Human-subject studies: observing and predicting human behaviour.
  • Brain imaging and neurological data: examining evidence about how the brain works.
Illustrative example

Suppose a researcher builds a model that attempts to reproduce the steps a person follows when solving a puzzle. The objective is to model the human thinking process, not merely to produce the correct answer.

3.4 Acting humanly

Acting humanly focuses on whether a machine's observable behaviour resembles human behaviour. The Turing Test is the main example associated with this approach.

Illustrative example

A conversational system is evaluated by asking whether a human interrogator can distinguish its written responses from the responses of another human.

3.5 Thinking rationally

Thinking rationally focuses on drawing correct conclusions using reasoning and logic. A logic-based AI system represents knowledge in a formal notation and applies deduction procedures to derive conclusions.

Worked reasoning example

Consider these two statements:

Premise 1: All humans are mortal.

Premise 2: Socrates is a human.

Therefore:

Conclusion: Socrates is mortal.

The conclusion follows from the premises using logical reasoning.

The lecture also identifies limitations of logic-based AI:

  • Representing real-world knowledge in formal logic can be difficult.
  • Finding a solution may require significant computation.
  • Uncertain real-world situations cannot always be handled by simple logical rules.

3.6 Acting rationally

Acting rationally means selecting actions that aim to achieve the best available outcome. This approach focuses on the design of intelligent agents.

Rational action is not necessarily the same as human-like action. A machine may use a method that humans would not normally use and still choose an appropriate action.

Illustrative example

A route-planning agent compares possible routes and selects one that best satisfies its objective. It does not need to imitate the way a human driver thinks.

3.7 How to distinguish them in an exam

USE THIS DECISION METHOD

First ask whether the question is about internal thought or observable action. Then ask whether the standard is human-like behaviour or rational correctness.

Question description Correct approach Reason
Models how people remember information. Thinking humanly It models internal human cognition.
Attempts to fool a human interrogator. Acting humanly It evaluates observable human-like behaviour.
Uses formal logic to derive a conclusion. Thinking rationally It focuses on correct reasoning.
Chooses an action that maximises a goal. Acting rationally It focuses on selecting an appropriate action.
04

ACTING HUMANLY

The Turing Test

4.1 Why was it introduced?

Alan Turing discussed the question of machine intelligence in his 1950 paper, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence". Rather than attempting to settle the abstract question "Can machines think?", the Turing Test provides an operational way to evaluate human-like conversational behaviour.

4.2 How does the test work?

  1. A human interrogator communicates through written questions.
  2. The interrogator receives written responses from a human and a machine.
  3. The interrogator attempts to determine which respondent is the machine.
  4. If the interrogator cannot reliably distinguish the machine's responses from the human's responses, the machine is considered to have passed the test.
Small example

The interrogator asks both participants: "What would you do if it started raining while you were walking to class?"

The interrogator compares the responses. The test is concerned with whether the machine's responses can be distinguished from human responses.

4.3 Four capabilities associated with the Turing Test

01

Natural Language Processing

Enables a computer to understand and communicate using human language.

02

Knowledge Representation

Allows information about the world to be represented in a form that a computer can use.

03

Automated Reasoning

Allows a computer to use available information to derive conclusions and answer questions.

04

Machine Learning

Allows a computer to improve its performance through learning from examples or experience.

4.4 Criticisms of the Turing Test

Passing the Turing Test does not automatically prove that a machine understands information or reasons in the same way as a human.

  • Human behaviour is not always intelligent. Imitating a human may also mean imitating human errors.
  • Intelligent behaviour need not be human-like. A system may solve a problem effectively without copying human behaviour.
  • Human observers can be fooled. Their expectations and assumptions influence their judgement.
  • Anthropomorphic fallacy. People have a tendency to attribute human characteristics to non-human systems.
  • Imitation is not necessarily understanding. A system can imitate intelligent behaviour using rules or patterns without demonstrating the kind of understanding people associate with intelligence.

4.5 The Chinese Room Argument

John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment (1980) questions whether following rules for manipulating symbols necessarily means that a system understands their meaning.

Understand the argument

Imagine a person inside a room who does not understand Chinese. The person receives Chinese symbols and follows a set of rules that tells them which symbols to return.

To an outside observer, the answers may appear meaningful. However, the person inside the room does not understand the Chinese language.

The argument challenges the assumption that producing appropriate responses necessarily demonstrates understanding.

Exam preparation: Be able to explain the purpose of the Turing Test, identify the four required AI capabilities, and discuss at least two criticisms.
05

RATIONAL DECISION-MAKING

Rationality, Utility and Optimisation

5.1 What is rationality?

Rationality concerns drawing sensible conclusions from facts, logic and available data, and choosing actions that are appropriate for the desired outcome.

In AI, acting rationally means attempting to achieve the best outcome according to the agent's objective and the information available.

5.2 Why does rational action involve optimisation?

To choose the best action, an agent needs a way to compare possible outcomes. This is where optimisation becomes useful.

The lecture introduces utility as a measure of the desirability of an outcome. An agent can use utility to compare how desirable different outcomes are.

SUPPLEMENTARY MATHEMATICAL FORMALISATION
U(outcome) = desirability of that outcome

Utility is a numerical representation of how desirable an outcome is for the agent. Higher utility represents a more desirable outcome under the chosen utility scale.

5.3 Expected utility under uncertainty

Sometimes an action can produce different outcomes. The agent may not know which outcome will occur. The lecture therefore introduces the idea of maximising expected utility.

The following equation is a supplementary mathematical way to express that idea. It is not printed as a formula in the introductory slides.

EXPECTED UTILITY
EU(a) = Σ P(oᵢ | a) × U(oᵢ)

Where:

  • EU(a): Expected utility of action a.
  • oᵢ: A possible outcome.
  • P(oᵢ | a): Probability of outcome oᵢ given action a.
  • U(oᵢ): Utility of that outcome.
  • Σ: Add the contributions from all possible outcomes.

5.4 Worked expected-utility example

Suppose an agent has two possible actions. Each action has two possible outcomes. The probabilities and utilities are given below.

Action Outcome Probability Utility
A Success 0.8 100
A Failure 0.2 0
B Success 0.5 120
B Failure 0.5 20

Step 1: Calculate expected utility of A

EU(A) = (0.8 × 100) + (0.2 × 0)

EU(A) = 80 + 0

EU(A) = 80

Step 2: Calculate expected utility of B

EU(B) = (0.5 × 120) + (0.5 × 20)

EU(B) = 60 + 10

EU(B) = 70

Step 3: Compare the actions

Action A has expected utility 80, while action B has expected utility 70. Under this utility model, action A is preferred.

IMPORTANT

This example illustrates expected utility using additional numerical values. It is not a numerical question copied from your lecture slides.

5.5 Advantages of optimisation

  • Generality: Optimisation is not limited to a fixed set of rules.
  • Practicality: It can be adapted to many real-world problems.
  • Established techniques: Solvers, simulation and experimentation are available.
  • Clear objectives: A problem can be described using a measurable objective.

5.6 Bounded rationality

A theoretical agent may attempt to maximise expected utility, but real agents have limits. They may not know everything about the environment and may not have unlimited computing resources.

Bounded rationality means that rational decision-making is subject to the agent's knowledge and computational constraints.

Example

A navigation agent may not be able to examine every possible route in the entire road network before giving a recommendation. It must make a decision using available information and computational resources.

5.7 Thinking rationally versus acting rationally

Thinking rationally Acting rationally
Focuses on reasoning and deriving correct conclusions. Focuses on choosing appropriate actions.
Often uses formal logic and deduction. Uses objectives, optimisation and utility.
Concerned with whether reasoning is correct. Concerned with whether actions achieve desirable outcomes.
06

CORE COMPONENT

Intelligent Agents

6.1 The basic idea

An agent is an entity that perceives and acts. It interacts with an environment by receiving percepts through sensors and performing actions through actuators.

AG
AGENT MODEL

Environment → Sensors → Percepts → Agent → Actions → Actuators → Environment

6.2 Understand each component

01

Environment

The world or surroundings in which the agent operates.

Example: The road network around a self-driving vehicle.

02

Sensors

Components used to receive information from the environment.

Example: Cameras and other sensing devices.

03

Percepts

The information the agent receives from its sensors at a particular time.

Example: A camera detects that a vehicle is in front.

04

Agent

The decision-making system that receives information and selects an action.

05

Actions

The operations selected by the agent to influence the environment.

Example: Slow down or change direction.

06

Actuators

Components through which the agent carries out its actions.

Example: The vehicle's steering, braking and acceleration systems.

6.3 The agent interaction cycle

  1. The environment has a current state.
  2. Sensors observe some information about that state.
  3. The agent receives a percept.
  4. The agent uses its program, knowledge or rules to select an action.
  5. Actuators perform the action.
  6. The environment changes, and the cycle can repeat.
IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Sensors are used to perceive. Actuators are used to act. Percepts are information received; actions are operations performed. Do not confuse these terms in examinations.

07

WORKED EXAMPLE

Trace a Simple Vacuum-Cleaner Agent

We will use a small two-room environment to follow the complete perceive-decide-act cycle. This is a supplementary teaching example, not code copied from the lecturer's slides.

7.1 Define the problem

A vacuum-cleaner agent can move between two rooms: A and B. Each room can be Dirty or Clean. The agent starts in room A.

Environment

Two rooms: A and B.

Percept

Current location and whether it is dirty.

Possible actions

SUCK, MOVE_RIGHT or STOP.

Goal

Clean both rooms.

7.2 Agent rules

  1. If the current room is Dirty, choose SUCK.
  2. Otherwise, if the agent is in A, choose MOVE_RIGHT.
  3. Otherwise, choose STOP.

7.3 Initial environment state

ROOM A DIRTY AGENT HERE
ROOM B DIRTY ---
CURRENT LOCATION A
PERCEPT A, Dirty
AGENT ACTION Not started

Initial state: the agent is in room A. Both rooms are dirty. Select Next Step to begin.

Step Location Percept Action Result
No actions performed yet.

7.4 Python implementation

The following Python function implements the three rules. The input is a percept containing the current location and status.

PYTHON vacuum_agent.py 
def vacuum_agent(percept):

    location, status = percept

    # Rule 1: clean the current room
    if status == "Dirty":
        return "SUCK"

    # Rule 2: move from A to B
    if location == "A":
        return "MOVE_RIGHT"

    # Rule 3: both rooms have been checked
    return "STOP"


# Test the agent
percept = ("A", "Dirty")

action = vacuum_agent(percept)

print("Percept:", percept)
print("Action:", action)

7.5 Trace the Python code manually

01

Receive the input

percept = ("A", "Dirty") means the agent is in room A and its current location is dirty.

02

Unpack the percept

location = "A" and status = "Dirty".

03

Evaluate the first condition

status == "Dirty" evaluates to True. The function returns "SUCK".

04

Why do the other rules not execute?

A return statement immediately exits the function. The MOVE_RIGHT and STOP conditions are not reached during this call.

EXPECTED OUTPUT

Percept: ('A', 'Dirty')
Action: SUCK

7.6 What does this example teach?

The agent is not simply performing random actions. It receives a percept, evaluates the information and selects an action according to its rules.

This small example demonstrates the fundamental interaction between an agent and its environment. More advanced AI algorithms will use more sophisticated decision-making methods.

Remember: The function uses the current percept to choose an action. The interactive trace above additionally models how the environment changes after the action.
08

AGENT DESIGN

PEAS, Task Environments and Agent Types

Before designing an agent, describe the task it faces. The exam asks you to write a PEAS description, classify an environment and justify each choice, and name the type of agent that fits.

8.1 Precise definitions

TermMeaning
PerceptThe agent's input at one instant.
Percept sequenceEverything the agent has perceived so far: P = [p0, p1, …, pt].
Agent functionThe abstract mapping f : P → a from a percept sequence to an action.
Agent programThe concrete code that implements f. Agent = architecture + program.
Rational agentChooses the action that maximises the expected value of its performance measure, given its percept sequence.

Why not a lookup table?

A table that lists an action for every possible percept sequence grows exponentially with the length of the sequence, so it can never be stored or filled in.

Rationality ≠ omniscience

An ideal agent would maximise the actual outcome, which needs knowledge of the future. A rational agent maximises the expected outcome, so it can still be unlucky.

Consequentialism

AI judges an agent by the consequences of what it does: the sequence of environment states its actions produce.

Performance-measure rule of thumb: measure what you want achieved in the environment, not how you think the agent should behave. “+1 each time a square is cleaned” rewards making a mess and cleaning it again; “+1 per clean square at each time step” rewards a clean floor.

8.2 PEAS descriptions

PEAS stands for Performance measure, Environment, Actuators and Sensors. Two examples from the course:

Automated taxiDelivery drone
PerformanceSafe, fast, legal, comfortable trip; profitProfit, delivery time, airspace laws, right location, package intact, no accidents, low noise, battery life
EnvironmentRoads, traffic, pedestrians, customersAirspace, obstacles (birds, buildings, poles), landing hazards, weather, routes, package weight
ActuatorsSteering, accelerator, brake, signal, hornPropellers, flight control, payload arm or claw, lights, delivery mechanism
SensorsCameras, LiDAR, speedometer, GPSGPS, radar or LiDAR, altimeter, barometer, gyroscope, accelerometer, camera, weight sensor

8.3 Classifying the environment

PropertyQuestion to ask
Fully vs partially observableDo the sensors give the complete relevant state at each moment?
Single vs multi-agentIs another entity best described as maximising its own performance measure? Another car is an agent; a telephone pole is part of the environment.
Deterministic vs stochasticIs the next state completely fixed by the current state and action? Stochastic means outcomes have probabilities.
Episodic vs sequentialDoes the current decision affect future decisions?
Static vs dynamicCan the world change while the agent is thinking? Semi-dynamic: the world waits but the score does not.
Discrete vs continuousAre states, time and actions countable?
Known vs unknownDoes the agent (or its designer) know the outcomes of its actions?

The hardest case is partially observable, multi-agent, stochastic, sequential, dynamic, continuous and unknown. This course assumes static, fully observable, deterministic and discrete environments.

8.4 Types of agent

01

Simple reflex agent

Acts on the current percept only, using condition–action rules. It ignores history.

02

Model-based reflex agent

Keeps internal state about the parts of the world it cannot see right now.

03

Goal-based (problem-solving) agent

Plans ahead by considering action sequences that reach a goal. That planning is search (Modules 02–04).

04

Utility-based agent

Chooses between goals and routes using a utility function, so it can trade off speed, safety and cost.

05

Learning agent

Improves its own performance from experience (Modules 07–08). Learning is optional for an agent, not required.

8.5 More facts worth quoting

  • Narrow AI solves one specific problem; AGI is a hypothetical agent that can learn any intellectual task a human can.
  • ELIZA (1964) imitated conversation with simple pattern matching, showing how easily people are fooled — one criticism of the Turing Test.
  • SAE self-driving levels: 1 driver assistance, 2 partial, 3 conditional, 4 high, 5 full automation.
  • Components of an agent: communicate with the environment (sensors and actuators), represent knowledge, reason and plan; learning is optional. ML ⊂ AI, and deep learning ⊂ ML.

IMPORTANT RELATIONSHIP

AI, Machine Learning and Deep Learning

9.1 Artificial Intelligence

AI is the broader field concerned with designing intelligent agents. An AI agent may involve perception, reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, goals and learning.

9.2 Machine Learning

Machine Learning focuses on learning from examples and data instead of relying entirely on explicitly programmed rules.

Machine Learning is part of AI, not a synonym for the whole field of AI.

9.3 Deep Learning

Deep Learning is a subfield of Machine Learning that uses layered artificial neural networks to learn representations from data.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

Designing intelligent agents

MACHINE LEARNING

Learning from examples and data

DEEP LEARNING

Layered artificial neural networks

9.4 Three types of Machine Learning

Learning type What the agent receives Example
Supervised learning Inputs with their correct outputs. Classifying an email as spam using labelled examples.
Unsupervised learning Inputs without supplied labels. Grouping customers into clusters.
Reinforcement learning Rewards or penalties after actions. Learning to play a game using rewards.

9.5 Test your classification

Identify the learning type in each scenario. Think about the type of feedback the learning system receives.

Scenario A: A model is trained using emails already labelled as spam or not spam.

Answer: Supervised learning, because the training examples have correct labels.

Scenario B: A bank groups customers without being given categories beforehand.

Answer: Unsupervised learning, because the inputs are not supplied with labels.

Scenario C: A robot receives a reward for successfully completing a task.

Answer: Reinforcement learning, because it learns from rewards or penalties.

10.2 What contributed to recent AI successes?

  • Faster computers and specialised hardware, including GPUs.
  • Large quantities of data from the internet and sensors.
  • Increased storage and cloud computing.
  • The dominance and progress of Machine Learning.
  • Improved optimisation methods, including Deep Learning.

10.3 Moravec's Paradox

Moravec's Paradox describes the observation that tasks which are difficult for computers may be easy for humans, while tasks that require little conscious effort from humans can be extremely difficult for machines.

Example

Computers can perform exceptionally well in games such as chess, but giving a robot the perception and mobility skills of a young child is much more difficult.

10.4 The AI Effect

The AI Effect describes how a task may stop being regarded as a demonstration of intelligence once a machine becomes good at performing it.

Example

Calculating and playing chess were once regarded as highly demanding intellectual activities. As computers became very capable at these tasks, people increasingly viewed them as computational problems.

Show suggested answer

Question 2 — AI approaches

Explain the difference between thinking rationally and acting rationally.

Show suggested answer

Question 3 — Intelligent agents

Explain the relationship between sensors, percepts, agents, actions and actuators.

Show suggested answer
