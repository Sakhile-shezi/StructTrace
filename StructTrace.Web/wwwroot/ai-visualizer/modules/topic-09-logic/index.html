KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATION

Introduction to First-Order Logic

First-order logic is a formal language used to represent objects, properties, relationships and statements about the world. It allows an AI system to represent facts and reason from those facts to reach conclusions.

In COMP304, the logic question focuses on three main tasks:

01

Translation

Convert an English statement into first-order logic.

02

Clause form

Convert logical statements into clauses in conjunctive normal form.

03

Resolution

Prove a conclusion by resolving clauses until the empty clause appears.

1.1 The building blocks of first-order logic

Element Meaning Example
Constant A specific object or individual. John, Anil, Peanuts, UKZN
Variable A symbol representing an object. x, y, z
Predicate A property or relationship. Student(x), Loves(x,y)
Function Maps an object to another object. MotherOf(x), f(x)
Universal quantifier For all objects. ∀x P(x)
Existential quantifier There exists at least one object. ∃x P(x)

1.2 Logical operators

¬ NOT

Negation

∧ AND

Conjunction

∨ OR

Disjunction

→ IMPLIES

Implication

↔ IF AND ONLY IF

Biconditional

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

A predicate such as Student(x) is not a complete statement until its variable is assigned or quantified. For example, ∀x Student(x) says that every object is a student, while ∃x Student(x) says that at least one object is a student.

02

ENGLISH TO LOGIC

Translating English into First-Order Logic

The first step is to identify who the statement talks about, what condition must hold, and what conclusion is being made.

2.1 The most important translation rules

EVERY / ALL / EACH ∀x (P(x) → Q(x))

If every object satisfying P also satisfies Q, use universal quantification and implication.

SOME / AT LEAST ONE ∃x (P(x) ∧ Q(x))

If at least one object has both properties, use existential quantification and AND.

NO ONE / NONE ∀x (P(x) → ¬Q(x))

No object satisfying P has property Q.

EVERYONE IS RELATED TO SOMEONE ∀x ∃y R(x,y)

For each x, there is at least one y related to that x.

2.2 Worked translations from your exam guide

EXAMPLE 1 — EVERY STUDENT WHO STUDIES PASSES

Identify the conditions:

Student(x) ∧ Studies(x)

The conclusion is:

Passes(x)

Because the statement says every student who studies:

∀x ((Student(x) ∧ Studies(x)) → Passes(x))
EXAMPLE 2 — SOME STUDENT PASSED

We need at least one object that is both a student and passed.

∃x (Student(x) ∧ Passed(x))
EXAMPLE 3 — NO STUDENT LIKES EXAMS

We are talking about every student, but the conclusion is negated.

∀x (Student(x) → ¬Likes(x, Exams))

An equivalent form is:

¬∃x (Student(x) ∧ Likes(x, Exams))

2.3 The UKZN examples

English statement First-order logic
Everyone at UKZN is smart. ∀x (At(x, UKZN) → Smart(x))
Someone at UKZN is smart. ∃x (At(x, UKZN) ∧ Smart(x))
No one at UKZN is lazy. ∀x (At(x, UKZN) → ¬Lazy(x))
COMMON TRANSLATION MISTAKES

∀x (At(x, UKZN) ∧ Smart(x)) says everyone is at UKZN and everyone is smart. That is different from saying everyone at UKZN is smart.

∃x (At(x, UKZN) → Smart(x)) is also wrong for "someone at UKZN is smart". The implication can be true simply because the person is not at UKZN.

03

QUANTIFIER SCOPE

Quantifier Order and Meaning

The order of quantifiers can change the meaning of a statement. This is especially important when translating statements involving "everyone" and "someone".

3.1 Two different statements

STATEMENT A
∀x ∃y Loves(x,y)

Everyone loves someone.

For each person x, there is at least one person y whom x loves.

STATEMENT B
∃y ∀x Loves(x,y)

There is someone whom everyone loves.

There is one particular person y whom every person x loves.

3.2 Why they are different

In Statement A, the person being loved can change depending on who is doing the loving. In Statement B, one individual must be loved by everybody.

EXAM RULE

∀x ∀y is equivalent to ∀y ∀x, and ∃x ∃y is equivalent to ∃y ∃x. But ∃x ∀y is not equivalent to ∀y ∃x.

3.3 Quantifier duality

UNIVERSAL QUANTIFIER DUALITY
∀x P(x) ≡ ¬∃x ¬P(x)

"Everything has property P" is equivalent to saying "there does not exist an object that lacks property P".

EXISTENTIAL QUANTIFIER DUALITY
∃x P(x) ≡ ¬∀x ¬P(x)

"Something has property P" is equivalent to saying "it is not true that everything lacks property P".

04

CONJUNCTIVE NORMAL FORM

Converting Logic into Clause Form

Clause form, also known as conjunctive normal form (CNF), represents a logical statement as a conjunction of clauses. A clause is a disjunction of literals.

4.1 What is a literal and a clause?

LITERAL
P(x)

A predicate or its negation.

CLAUSE
¬P(x) ∨ Q(x)

A disjunction of literals.

CNF
(A ∨ B) ∧ (C ∨ D)

Conjunction of clauses.

4.2 The six-step conversion procedure

01

Eliminate implications and biconditionals

Replace → and ↔ using logical equivalences.

P → Q ≡ ¬P ∨ Q
02

Move negations inward

Apply De Morgan's laws and quantifier negation rules.

¬∀x P ≡ ∃x ¬P
03

Standardise variables

Give each quantifier its own variable name.

04

Skolemise

Replace existential variables with Skolem constants or functions.

05

Drop universal quantifiers

Universal quantifiers are understood implicitly in clauses.

06

Distribute OR over AND

Separate the resulting conjunction into individual clauses.

A ∨ (B ∧ C) ≡ (A ∨ B) ∧ (A ∨ C)
KEEP THIS ORDER

Follow the six steps in order. Do not Skolemise before removing implications, moving negations inward and standardising the variables.

4.3 Logical equivalences to remember

Original form Equivalent form
P → Q ¬P ∨ Q
P ↔ Q (P → Q) ∧ (Q → P)
¬∀x P(x) ∃x ¬P(x)
¬∃x P(x) ∀x ¬P(x)
¬(P ∧ Q) ¬P ∨ ¬Q
¬(P ∨ Q) ¬P ∧ ¬Q
¬¬P P
P ∨ (Q ∧ R) (P ∨ Q) ∧ (P ∨ R)
05

EXISTENTIAL ELIMINATION

Skolemisation: Constants and Functions

Skolemisation removes existential quantifiers by replacing their variables with new symbols. The correct replacement depends on whether the existential variable is inside the scope of a universal quantifier.

5.1 When to use a Skolem constant

NO DEPENDENT UNIVERSAL VARIABLE
∃x Student(x)

There exists at least one student. We can represent this unknown individual using a new constant, such as A.

Student(A)

5.2 When to use a Skolem function

EXISTENTIAL VARIABLE INSIDE A UNIVERSAL SCOPE
∀x ∃y Loves(x,y)

For every x, some y is loved by x. The object y may be different for each x.

Therefore, replace y with a function of x:

∀x Loves(x, f(x))

5.3 Compare the two cases

Expression Replacement Reason
∃x P(x) P(A) No preceding universal variable.
∀x ∃y R(x,y) R(x, f(x)) y can depend on x.
∀x ∀z ∃y R(x,z,y) R(x,z, f(x,z)) y can depend on both x and z.
IMPORTANT EXAM MISTAKE

Do not replace an existential variable inside ∀x with a constant if its value depends on x. Use a Skolem function f(x). Otherwise, you incorrectly force the same object to serve every x.

06

STEP-BY-STEP CONVERSION

Worked Clause-Form Examples

Example 1 — Remove an implication and distribute

GIVEN
∀x ((P(x) ∨ Q(x)) → R(x))
STEP 1 — REMOVE IMPLICATION
¬(P(x) ∨ Q(x)) ∨ R(x)
STEP 2 — APPLY DE MORGAN'S LAW
(¬P(x) ∧ ¬Q(x)) ∨ R(x)
STEP 3 — DISTRIBUTE OR OVER AND
(¬P(x) ∨ R(x)) ∧ (¬Q(x) ∨ R(x))
FINAL CLAUSES
C1: ¬P(x) ∨ R(x)
C2: ¬Q(x) ∨ R(x)

Example 2 — Student takes a course

GIVEN
∀x (Student(x) → ∃y (Course(y) ∧ Takes(x,y)))
STEP 1 — REMOVE IMPLICATION
∀x (¬Student(x) ∨ ∃y (Course(y) ∧ Takes(x,y)))
STEP 2 — SKOLEMISE

Because y occurs within the scope of ∀x, use a Skolem function f(x).

¬Student(x) ∨ (Course(f(x)) ∧ Takes(x,f(x)))
STEP 3 — DISTRIBUTE
C1: ¬Student(x) ∨ Course(f(x))
C2: ¬Student(x) ∨ Takes(x,f(x))

Example 3 — Every bird flies

GIVEN
¬∃x (Bird(x) ∧ ¬Flies(x))
STEP 1 — MOVE NEGATION INWARD
∀x ¬(Bird(x) ∧ ¬Flies(x))
STEP 2 — APPLY DE MORGAN'S LAW
∀x (¬Bird(x) ∨ Flies(x))
FINAL CLAUSE
¬Bird(x) ∨ Flies(x)

6.1 The lecture's longer Skolemisation example

EXPRESSION FROM THE EXAM GUIDE
∀x [∀y (Animal(y) → Loves(x,y))] → [∃y Loves(y,x)]

The guide's final clause-form result is:

C1: Animal(F(x)) ∨ Loves(G(x), x)
C2: ¬Loves(x, F(x)) ∨ Loves(G(x), x)

The Skolem functions are dependent on x because the existential choices occur within the scope of the universal variable.

AUTOMATED THEOREM PROVING

Resolution: Proving a Statement

Resolution is an inference rule used to prove whether a conclusion follows from a set of facts. It works by adding the negation of the goal and deriving a contradiction.

7.1 The resolution principle

RESOLUTION RULE
(P ∨ A), (¬P ∨ B)
Therefore: (A ∨ B)

The complementary literals P and ¬P are eliminated. The remaining literals are combined into a new clause.

7.2 What is unification?

Before two clauses can be resolved, their complementary predicates may need to be made identical by substituting variables. This process is called unification.

EXAMPLE
Likes(x, Peanuts)
¬Likes(John, y)

Substitution:

{x/John, y/Peanuts}

After substitution, the literals become Likes(John, Peanuts) and ¬Likes(John, Peanuts), so they are complementary.

7.3 The four main proof stages

01

Represent the facts

Write the facts and rules in first-order logic.

02

Convert to clauses

Put all facts and rules into clause form.

03

Negate the goal

Add the negation of what you want to prove.

04

Resolve

Unify complementary literals and derive the empty clause.

THE EMPTY CLAUSE

The empty clause, written as □, represents a contradiction. If resolution produces □, the negated goal is inconsistent with the facts. Therefore, the original goal follows from the facts.

7.4 How to record a resolution proof

Step Resolve Substitution Result
1 Clause A with Clause B {x/John} New resolvent
2 Previous result with Clause C {y/Peanuts} New resolvent
3 Previous result with Clause D — □
EXAM MARKS

Always show which clauses you resolve, the substitution used and the resulting clause. Your guide specifically warns against leaving out substitutions.

PAST EXAM REVISION QUESTION

Resolution Proof: West Is a Criminal

This is the longer resolution problem in your exam guide. It combines facts, rules, an existential statement, Skolemisation, clause conversion and a multi-step proof.

9.1 The problem statement

The law says it is a crime for an American to sell weapons to hostile nations. Nono is an enemy of America. Nono owns missiles, and all of Nono's missiles were sold to it by Colonel West, who is American. An enemy of America counts as hostile. Prove that West is a criminal.

9.2 First-order logic facts

1 ∀x∀y∀z ((American(x) ∧ Weapon(y) ∧ Sells(x,y,z) ∧ Hostile(z)) → Criminal(x))
2–3 ∃x (Owns(Nono,x) ∧ Missile(x))
4 ∀x ((Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono,x)) → Sells(West,x,Nono))
5 ∀x (Enemy(x,America) → Hostile(x))
6 American(West)
7 Enemy(Nono,America)
8 ∀x (Missile(x) → Weapon(x))

9.3 Skolemise the existential statement

∃x (Owns(Nono,x) ∧ Missile(x))

There is an object owned by Nono that is a missile. Since there is no preceding universal variable, introduce a Skolem constant M1.

Owns(Nono,M1)
Missile(M1)

9.4 Clause form

C1 ¬American(x) ∨ ¬Weapon(y) ∨ ¬Sells(x,y,z) ∨ ¬Hostile(z) ∨ Criminal(x)
C2Owns(Nono,M1)
C3Missile(M1)
C4 ¬Missile(x) ∨ ¬Owns(Nono,x) ∨ Sells(West,x,Nono)
C5 ¬Enemy(x,America) ∨ Hostile(x)
C6American(West)
C7Enemy(Nono,America)
C8 ¬Missile(x) ∨ Weapon(x)

9.5 Negate the goal

The goal is Criminal(West). Add its negation:

C9: ¬Criminal(West)

9.6 Interactive resolution proof

PROOF STATUS Ready

C9: ¬Criminal(West)

Follow all nine resolution steps to prove the goal.
FINAL LOGIC CONCLUSION

The resolution proof derives the empty clause. Therefore, the negation of Criminal(West) is false, and West is a criminal.

PAST PAPER · NOVEMBER 2024 Q5

Semantics and the 2024 Panther Proof

Every paper has a 15-mark logic question in three parts: translate a statement (2), convert everything to clause form (5) and prove a goal by resolution (8). This section works through the 2024 question and adds the semantics you need to explain a proof.

10.1 What makes a sentence true

Model

A non-empty domain of objects plus the relations and functions among them.

Interpretation

Maps constant symbols to objects, predicate symbols to relations and function symbols to functions.

Brother(Richard, John) is true because ⟨Richard, John⟩ is in the brother relation.

Equality

Says two terms refer to the same object. Sibling needs it: ∀x,y Sibling(x, y) ⇔ [¬(x = y) ∧ ∃m,f ¬(m = f) ∧ Parent(m, x) ∧ Parent(f, x) ∧ Parent(m, y) ∧ Parent(f, y)].

10.2 The 2024 question

#Statement
1∀x CarnivorousCat(x) ⇒ ∃y (isAMountain(y) ∧ livesOn(x, y))
2∀x panther(x) ⇒ CarnivorousCat(x)
3∀x panther(x) ⇒ Nocturnal(x)
4∀x ∀y isAMountain(y) ∧ CarnivorousCat(x) ∧ Nocturnal(x) ∧ livesOn(x, y) ⇒ hasHerbivores(y)
5∀x ∀y livesOn(x, y) ∧ hasHerbivores(y) ⇒ huntsRabbits(x)
Q5.1 Translation of statement 4

For every x and y: if y is a mountain and x is a nocturnal carnivorous cat that lives on y, then y has herbivores.

10.3 Clause form (Q5.2)

CC abbreviates CarnivorousCat. Statement 1's ∃y sits inside ∀x, so it becomes a Skolem function f(x), and its conjunction splits into two clauses.

10.4 Exam tips for every resolution question

  • Number every clause and give the substitution at every step.
  • Negating a universal goal: ¬∀x (P(x) ⇒ Q(x)) ≡ ∃x (P(x) ∧ ¬Q(x)). Skolemise with a fresh constant and split into two clauses.
  • Split conjunctions: a fact such as Loser(Tony) ∧ Loser(Tom) is two clauses. A biconditional becomes two clauses.
  • Merge duplicate literals in a resolvent, as in steps 2, 3 and 6 above.

EXAM ERROR PREVENTION

Common Logic Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Using AND for every universal statement
∀x (Student(x) ∧ Passes(x))
∀x (Student(x) → Passes(x))

The universal statement normally uses implication to express "every student who satisfies the condition also passes".

Mistake 2 — Using implication for an existential statement
∃x (Student(x) → Passed(x))
∃x (Student(x) ∧ Passed(x))

The existential statement requires an object that satisfies both conditions.

Mistake 3 — Using a constant when a function is required
∀x ∃y Loves(x,y) → Loves(x,A)
∀x Loves(x,f(x))

The existential choice may depend on x. Use a Skolem function when it is inside the universal scope.

Mistake 4 — Forgetting to negate the goal

Add the negation of the conclusion before resolving. Otherwise, the proof does not follow the resolution-by- contradiction method.

Mistake 5 — Leaving out substitutions

Write the variable bindings beside the resolution step, for example {x/Peanuts}, {y/Anil}.

Mistake 6 — Stopping before the empty clause

A proof is complete when the resolvents produce the empty clause □.
